**Status:** Complete (v1.0)
**Related documents:** `README.md`, `docs/decisions.md`, `docs/flow.md`, `docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md`

## 1. Origin and Motivation

The project began not as a research exercise but as a systems-engineering question: what does it actually take to move a motor imagery brain-computer interface (BCI) from an offline classification benchmark to something that behaves like a real-time decision system? Academic BCI work overwhelmingly reports offline, cross-validated accuracy on pre-segmented trials. That number answers a narrower question than the one that matters for any assistive application — a wheelchair controller, a communication device, a neurorehabilitation tool — none of which have the luxury of a human already having told the system when a trial begins and ends.

The guiding motivation, established early and carried through every subsequent phase, was to build and honestly characterize a **real-time closed-loop pipeline**: raw signal in, decoded intent out, with every step of that path — buffering, filtering, inference, latency, and failure modes — built and measured explicitly, not assumed. The assistive-device framing (a device that could, in principle, sit between an EEG headset and a wheelchair or communication interface) was adopted deliberately partway through the project, once the offline model was validated, because it forces harder, more honest questions than a pure classification benchmark does: What is the actual latency budget? What happens when the model is uncertain? What happens to a user who doesn't calibrate well?

## 2. Research Backing

The project is built on two established pieces of published work, not invented from scratch:

- **Dataset**: BCI Competition IV Dataset 2a (Brunner, Leeb, Müller-Putz, Schlögl, Pfurtscheller, 2008) — 9 subjects, 4-class motor imagery (left hand, right hand, feet, tongue), 22 EEG channels, accessed through the MOABB (Mother of All BCI Benchmarks) framework, which standardizes dataset loading and epoch extraction across the BCI research community.
- **Model architecture**: EEGNet (Lawhern et al., 2018) — a compact convolutional architecture purpose-built for EEG, using temporal convolution followed by depthwise spatial convolution and separable temporal processing. Chosen specifically because its inductive bias (learn temporal filters, then spatial filters per temporal filter) mirrors what BCI researchers do by hand with classical pipelines, while remaining small enough (3,444 parameters) to train reliably on the limited number of trials available per subject (~288 per session).

Beyond these two anchors, published literature informed several concrete decisions during the project, not just at the start:

- The decision to trust the dataset's documented `interval = (2, 6)` seconds — rather than the naive assumption that a trial's classifiable window begins at the marker onset — came from recognizing that the BCI IV-2a protocol places a 2-second preparatory period (fixation cross, then cue) before motor imagery actually begins.
- The expectation that real-time online accuracy would sit meaningfully below offline accuracy was informed by comparison against published real-time 4-class motor imagery systems on comparable consumer-grade hardware, which report online accuracies in the 35–41% range — a benchmark the final ~61–65% figure was measured against, not assumed to need matching.
- The recognition that subjects 5 and 6 performing near chance is a documented, cross-study phenomenon in this exact dataset (BCI illiteracy), not an artifact of this pipeline, was confirmed against independent published results before being accepted as an explanation rather than treated as a bug to keep chasing.

## 3. Value Proposition and Industry Standing

An independent technical review commissioned after project completion (see `docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md` for the full text and a point-by-point response) characterized the result as **"a strong research-engineering prototype, not a commercial BCI stack"** — and that framing is accurate and worth stating plainly rather than inflating. The review's overall assessment placed the project at a level appropriate for a junior-to-mid BCI software/ML engineering role, with a clear, specific list of gaps separating it from production-grade neurotechnology: no classical baseline (CSP/Riemannian) to contextualize the deep-learning result, no cross-subject generalization strategy, a non-causal filter in the real-time path, and a validation methodology (test-set-driven checkpoint selection) that inflates the reported offline number.

Those gaps are real, and they are documented rather than concealed — both in the README's existing "Limitations & Future Work" section and, more thoroughly, in `docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md`. The value of the project, in its current state, is not as a deployable clinical system but as a demonstration of **systems thinking applied to a research problem**: taking a model that performs well offline and rigorously, honestly characterizing what happens to it under the actual constraints of real-time operation — no future data, finite compute budget, no ground truth at inference time, per-user signal variability — rather than stopping at the offline number.

## 4. Build Journey

### 4.1 Phase 1 — Environment and Structure

The project began with a deliberately boring but important step: a locked-version `requirements.txt`, a clean `src/` folder structure separating training, export, streaming, inference, and (later) app code, and a `.gitignore` excluding large binary artifacts (`data/`, `models/`, `venv/`) from version control. This decision paid off directly later — every phase that followed depended on a stable, reproducible base rather than an ad hoc script collection.

### 4.2 Phase 2 — Offline Training

EEGNet was implemented from the published architecture and trained on Subject 1 using the dataset's native session-based split (`0train` vs. `1test`), rather than a random split, specifically to avoid information leakage between what would become the calibration and evaluation sessions — a decision aligned with how the original BCI competition protocol was designed to be evaluated. This produced a first working result: 74.3% validation accuracy on 4-class motor imagery, well above the 25% chance baseline.

It was only much later, in response to external review, that a genuine flaw in this training loop was recognized: the checkpoint-saving logic selected the best-performing epoch by repeatedly evaluating against the same held-out test set used for final reporting. This means the reported 74.3% (and the later multi-subject numbers) were not clean, single-shot held-out estimates — they reflected a mild form of test-set leakage through model selection. Unlike the project's other documented limitations, this was treated as a bug requiring correction rather than a tradeoff to accept and explain — the full account of that correction, including a proposed shortcut that was explicitly rejected, is given in Section 4.9.

Multi-subject training (all 9 subjects) was deliberately deferred at this point in favor of moving forward with a single subject, and was completed later, out of the original sequence, once the real-time pipeline was stable — a scope-management decision revisited explicitly in Section 4.6.

### 4.3 Phase 3 — Export and Latency Benchmarking

The trained PyTorch model was exported to ONNX for deployment. This step surfaced an early, low-stakes but instructive debugging episode: the newer PyTorch export path (`torch.export`-based, dynamo) natively produced opset 18 graphs, while the export was initially requested at opset 17, triggering an internal downgrade attempt that failed on a `Pad` operator with no version-17 adapter, then silently fell back to opset 18 with a fallback warning. The fix was simply to request opset 18 directly rather than rely on the fallback — a small but useful lesson in reading tool warnings carefully rather than assuming an error block means failure.

Numerical verification (PyTorch vs. ONNX Runtime outputs on real test trials) showed a maximum logit difference of 2.86 × 10⁻⁶ — effectively floating-point noise — confirming the export was trustworthy before any further work was built on top of it. A subsequent latency benchmark compared PyTorch CPU, PyTorch MPS (Apple Silicon GPU), and ONNX Runtime CPU across 200 single-trial inference calls. The result was genuinely counter-intuitive: ONNX Runtime CPU (0.29 ms mean, 0.32 ms P95) outperformed both PyTorch backends, including MPS (0.93 ms) — for a model this small, GPU dispatch overhead dominated any compute advantage, while ONNX Runtime's static-graph optimizations minimized overhead entirely. This became the basis for selecting ONNX Runtime CPU as the production inference backend.

### 4.4 Phase 4 — Real-Time Streaming Simulation

An LSL (Lab Streaming Layer) outlet was built to replay the dataset's continuous, unsegmented EEG — deliberately choosing continuous streaming with realistic inter-trial gaps over simpler pre-cut trial replay, since the goal was to force the downstream pipeline to detect trial timing itself, the way a real headset would present data. A parallel marker stream carried ground-truth event labels, useful for development and validation, understood from the outset as a scaffold that would not exist in true deployment (addressed directly in Section 4.7).

A wall-clock pacing bug surfaced here: the initial channel-metadata-reading code, which queried `resolve_streams()`'s lightweight stream summaries rather than a fully-connected inlet's `.info()`, intermittently returned empty channel lists due to a race condition in when LSL populates full stream description metadata. The fix — retrying with a connected inlet's `.info()` in a bounded loop — became the standard defensive pattern used throughout the rest of the project wherever LSL stream metadata was read.

### 4.5 Phase 5 — The Real-Time Accuracy Investigation (the central engineering saga of the project)

This phase is the technical heart of the project and deserves the fullest account, because nearly every non-trivial bug in the entire codebase originated or was diagnosed here.

**The starting point** was a naive real-time pipeline: buffer the last 4 seconds of signal, feed it unfiltered directly to the ONNX model, and compare against whatever marker had most recently fired. This produced **34.5% accuracy** — barely above chance, despite using the same model that scored 74.3% offline.

**First hypothesis and fix — missing bandpass filtering.** Training data had been implicitly bandpass-filtered (4–38 Hz) by MOABB before epoching; the real-time buffer was raw. Adding a Butterworth bandpass filter, applied via zero-phase `filtfilt`, raised accuracy to **46.9%** — a real improvement, but still far short of the offline number, and far short of what the fix should plausibly have produced.

**Second hypothesis and fix — wrong scoring window.** The evaluation logic treated any window falling within 4 seconds of a marker's onset as valid ground truth. Investigation into MOABB's documentation revealed that `tmin`/`tmax` for the `MotorImagery` paradigm are defined relative to the *dataset's own task interval*, not the raw marker timestamp — and the BCI IV-2a protocol places actual motor imagery **2 to 6 seconds** after the cue, not 0 to 4. This was confirmed directly by querying `dataset.interval` in code rather than trusting memory or documentation prose, and the result — `(2, 6)` — corrected a scoring window that had been silently comparing predictions to the wrong 4-second slice of every trial for most of the debugging so far. Correcting this raised accuracy to roughly 53–54%.

**Third hypothesis and fix — filter edge distortion.** Even with correct trial alignment, individual trials showed a striking pattern: entire trials being confidently misclassified into the same wrong class throughout, rather than scattered, low-confidence errors — a signature of a systematic input-processing problem rather than genuine classification difficulty. The cause was traced to `filtfilt`'s handling of window edges: offline, MOABB filters the entire continuous recording once, so every training epoch has real signal on both sides of it; the real-time pipeline was filtering a freshly-cut 4-second snapshot each cycle, forcing `filtfilt` to pad the leading edge with reflected data rather than genuine history. Extending the rolling buffer by one extra second of real history, filtering the full extended window, then cropping off the distorted leading margin, raised accuracy to **61.3% (full scoring window) / 65.1% (near-end, tightest-alignment window)**.

**A dead end, reported honestly.** A follow-up experiment tested whether adding a *trailing* margin (accepting an additional 0.5 seconds of latency so `filtfilt` would have genuine data on both edges, not just the leading one) would close the remaining gap further. The result was inconclusive and mildly negative on the tightest-alignment metric — full-window accuracy nudged up marginally, near-end accuracy went down — within the range explainable by sampling noise given the small number of scored predictions per short test run. This was not pursued further; the added latency was not repaid by a reproducible accuracy gain, and the leading-margin-only version was kept as the final, documented real-time configuration.

**The remaining gap was accepted as structural, not a defect to keep chasing.** Real-time inference fundamentally cannot access future data, which zero-phase filtering requires to avoid edge distortion entirely. This is stated plainly in the README rather than framed as solved, and is directly addressed in `docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md` as the review's most emphatic, and fairest, technical criticism.

**Multi-subject training was completed at this point**, out of original sequence, once the real-time pipeline's correctness was established — producing an initial offline benchmark table (mean 62.9% ± 15.3%, range 38.9%–81.9% across 9 subjects) and confirming, against independently published results on this exact dataset, that subjects 5 and 6 performing near chance is a known, cross-study pattern (BCI illiteracy) rather than a pipeline artifact. This table was later found to be invalid and corrected — see Section 4.9.

### 4.9 A Post-Completion Correction: Discovering and Fixing Test-Set Leakage

After the project was substantially complete, an independent technical review identified a genuine methodological flaw in the Phase 2 training loop: checkpoint selection during training evaluated candidate epochs against the same held-out session (`1test`) that was later reported as the final accuracy figure. This is test-set leakage through model selection, and it meant every offline accuracy number produced up to that point — including the 74.3% Subject 1 baseline the entire Phase 5 real-time investigation was built against — was not a clean, valid held-out estimate.

This was treated as a bug to fix, not a limitation to document around. The fix required a genuine three-way split: `0train` was split internally (80/20, stratified) into an inner-train and inner-validation set; checkpoint selection during training now uses only the inner-validation split; the true test set, `1test`, is evaluated exactly once, after training is fully complete. Retraining all 9 subjects under this corrected methodology produced a substantially different, and more informative, result: mean accuracy dropped to 53.1% ± 18.4% — but not uniformly. Subjects 1, 3, 8, and 9 (strong signal) dropped modestly. Subjects 5 and 6 (already near chance) barely moved. Subjects 2 and 7, previously reported as solid mid-tier performers (57.6%, 61.8%), collapsed to near-chance (28.1%, 33.7%).

A follow-up refinement recovered some of this gap legitimately: the inner-validation split had also reduced available training data by 20%, a separate inefficiency from the leakage itself. A two-stage refit procedure — determine the best stopping epoch via the honest validation split, then retrain from scratch on the *full* training session using that stopping point, evaluated once against the test set — recovered several points across most subjects without reintroducing leakage (the test set is still touched exactly once, by a model that never saw it during training or selection). This refit, run twice independently for stability, produced the project's final reported numbers: mean 59.5% ± 16.9%, with Subject 9 emerging as the strongest performer (77.3%), superseding Subject 3 as the live-demo model.

The two-subject divergence under correction turned out to be genuinely informative rather than a footnote: Subject 7 recovered almost fully once given the full training set (68.8%, close to its original leaky number) — evidence its collapse was a data-starvation artifact, not illusory signal. Subject 2 did not recover (35.3% regardless of data volume) — evidence its original "good" score was leakage-driven illusion rather than real, if data-hungry, signal. Distinguishing these two failure modes was only possible because the correction was pursued rigorously rather than accepted as a single aggregate drop.

One proposal was explicitly considered and rejected during this process: taking the higher of two independent training runs' results per subject, on the reasoning that "every run gives new results, so there's no illegitimacy in picking the best." This was rejected because it reproduces the identical leakage mechanism just fixed, relocated from "which epoch" to "which training run" — repeatedly evaluating against a fixed test set and keeping the most favorable outcome inflates the reported number regardless of whether any single run was itself honestly conducted. The final numbers use the mean across independent runs, not the maximum.

The corrected weights required re-exporting every ONNX model and re-verifying real-time accuracy against the new Subject 1 checkpoint, which was itself an instructive final debugging pass: `sanity_check_aligned_v3.py`, unmodified since Phase 5, referenced the pre-Phase-6.5 generic LSL stream name (`"SimulatedEEG"`) and a hardcoded Subject 3 ONNX path, both stale relative to changes made in later phases — caught and fixed the same way as every other issue in this project, by checking the actual file content rather than assuming which version was current.

### 4.6 Phase 6 — The Live Dashboard

The dashboard was built incrementally: a functional skeleton showing live predictions (Step 6.1), ground-truth comparison and running accuracy (6.2), a live scrolling waveform of the actual filtered signal driving inference (6.3), and a full visual redesign (6.4) after early feedback that the initial layout was underdeveloped and — in one specific case — contained a real bug: using `st.container(border=True)` inside a per-cycle update loop caused new copies of the same card to stack indefinitely rather than overwrite in place, producing an endless-scrolling page. The fix — rendering all per-cycle dynamic content exclusively through `st.empty()` placeholders — became a standing rule for the rest of the dashboard's development.

A subject switcher (Step 6.5) turned the dashboard from a fixed single-subject demo into a tool that could launch and manage its own `lsl_outlet.py` subprocess per subject selection, coupling "which subject's EEG is streamed" and "which subject's model interprets it" as one atomic action rather than two independently-managed pieces that could silently drift apart.

**Six device-readiness features** were then added in sequence: prediction smoothing (exponential-moving-average over recent softmax outputs, reducing frame-to-frame flicker), confidence-gated rejection (withholding a decision below a tunable confidence threshold, directly motivated by the assistive-device framing — a wheelchair should not act on an uncertain signal), a live confusion matrix and per-class precision/recall, a command-mapping panel translating classes into device actions with a simple animated schematic, session logging (per-cycle CSV plus a cross-session summary history), a per-channel signal-quality indicator (explicitly documented as a variance-based proxy, since no true electrode impedance is available from simulated data), and an accessibility mode (larger text, higher contrast, reduced motion).

Two further bugs surfaced and were fixed during this stretch. First, a naming collision: `time.sleep(0.25)` appeared twice in the manual update loop (once in the "still buffering" branch, once at the true end of the cycle), and session-logging code was mistakenly inserted at the wrong occurrence, referencing variables (`display_label`, `gt_label`) that did not yet exist in that branch — a `NameError` that was root-caused by locating every occurrence of the ambiguous anchor line rather than guessing which one was intended. Second, and more structurally, toggling accessibility mode caused the confusion-matrix panel to visibly duplicate with reduced opacity and never fully clear — traced to the manual `for` loop plus `time.sleep()` plus `st.rerun()` pattern fighting against Streamlit's own widget-triggered reruns. The fix was a genuine architectural correction, not a patch: migrating the entire live-update loop to Streamlit's `@st.fragment(run_every=...)` decorator, which is purpose-built for exactly this "auto-refresh part of a page independently of full-page reruns" use case, and which also allowed simplifying and better-aligning the overall layout in the same pass.

### 4.7 Extending to a Forensics Tool, and Merging Two Codebases

An offline replay and forensics tool was developed independently (in a separate directory) as an extension for debugging recorded sessions without needing a live stream — and, when reviewed, was found to have **independently reintroduced two of the exact bugs already diagnosed and fixed in Phase 5**: a naive "most recent marker before now" ground-truth lookup with no time-window cutoff (the same rest-period contamination bug fixed in Step 5.3), and a clock-unit mismatch where recorded marker timestamps used LSL wall-clock time while replay indexing used dataset-relative sample position — two different time bases being compared directly, which meant ground truth was very likely never actually aligning correctly during any of that tool's early testing.

Merging the two codebases into a single repository and a single multi-page Streamlit application (`src/app/Live_Monitor.py` plus `src/app/pages/`) surfaced a long sequence of path-resolution and file-tracking issues, each diagnosed by direct verification rather than repeated guessing: a `git mv` on an untracked file failing silently with a `fatal:` error easy to miss in scrollback; relative import paths breaking when files moved one directory level deeper; a hardcoded `--subject 3` in a "Record new session" button silently recording the wrong subject's data whenever a different subject was live in the other tab (fixed by reading the actively-connected subject from Streamlit's shared cross-page session state rather than a hardcoded default); and, separately, an emoji-containing filename (`1_🔬_Forensics_Replay.py`) failing to move correctly through a terminal command due to shell encoding handling, resolved by switching to a plain-ASCII filename.

A genuine constants-duplication risk was addressed directly during this merge by extracting every shared value — channel lists, filter parameters, the validated `(2, 6)` second scoring interval, file paths — into a single module, `src/common/bci_utils.py`, imported by every component of the system rather than redefined per file. This was a direct, deliberate response to having just observed what duplication costs: the forensics tool's regressed bugs existed specifically because its logic had drifted out of sync with fixes already made elsewhere.

### 4.8 Phase 7 — Documentation and a Genuine Git Recovery

Writing the README surfaced its own instructive sequence of Git issues, arising specifically from editing the same file (`README.md`) in two places — locally in an editor and directly through GitHub's web interface — without pulling between edits. This produced, in order: a rejected push from a diverged history, a Mermaid-diagram-referencing project-structure block that failed to render due to markdown fence nesting, a duplicate full copy of the README's contents concatenated after a merge, and finally a set of orphaned merge-conflict markers (`=======`, `>>>>>>> Stashed changes`) left in the file after a partially-manual conflict resolution, located precisely via `grep` for conflict-marker syntax before any further edits were attempted, rather than assumed fixed. Alongside this, a set of source files from earlier phases (`eegnet.py`, `lsl_outlet.py`, and several `sanity_check_*.py` debugging scripts) were discovered to have never actually been committed to version control, despite terminal output at the time appearing to show successful commits — verified directly via `git log -- <file>` returning no history, rather than assumed from memory of the session.

## 5. Findings and Results

**Offline classification (per-subject, session-based split, all 9 BCI IV-2a subjects, corrected leak-free methodology — see Section 4.9):**
Mean accuracy 59.5% ± 16.9% (SD across 9 subjects, each measured across 2 independent training runs), range 35.3%–77.3%. Subject 9 (77.3%) was selected for the live demo, superseding the originally-used Subject 3 once the corrected numbers showed them statistically close together with Subject 9 slightly ahead. Subject 1 (75.4% mean, corrected) was the subject re-validated for real-time accuracy following the methodology correction. Subjects 5 and 6 scoring near chance replicates an independently published pattern for this exact dataset, not a pipeline defect — though the underlying cause (signal-to-noise ratio, individual motor imagery strategy, electrode-level factors) was not further investigated, a gap explicitly acknowledged in `docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md`.

**Inference latency (ONNX Runtime, CPU, single-trial, n=200):** 0.29 ms mean, 0.32 ms P95 — leaving effectively the entire real-time latency budget to signal acquisition and windowing rather than model compute. This number is explicitly scoped in `docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md` as *compute* latency only, not total system decision latency, in direct response to external review feedback distinguishing the two.

**Real-time vs. offline accuracy (Subject 1, corrected methodology, extended ~15-minute validation run):** 75.4% offline (mean of 2 runs) vs. 63.8% full scoring window (868/1360 scored predictions) / 60.5% precision window (202/334 scored predictions) real-time — an ~12-15 point gap, closely matching the magnitude measured against the original (pre-correction) model, which is itself informative: it indicates the real-time/offline gap is a structural property of the filtering approach, independent of which specific model weights are underneath it, rather than an artifact of the leakage issue that was separately fixed.

**System architecture, as built:** continuous LSL streaming → thread-safe rolling buffer → leading-margin bandpass filtering → ONNX Runtime inference → exponential-moving-average smoothing → confidence-gated rejection → device-command mapping, running inside a single unified Streamlit application with both a live monitoring mode and an offline forensics/replay mode, sharing one configuration source of truth.

## 6. External Validation

An independent technical review of the completed repository (`docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md`) assessed the project as a strong research-engineering prototype suitable as a hiring signal for a junior-to-mid BCI software/ML engineering role, while identifying specific, technically substantive gaps separating it from production readiness: absence of a classical (CSP) or geometric (Riemannian) baseline to contextualize the deep-learning result, no cross-subject generalization or domain-adaptation strategy, continued use of a non-causal filter in the real-time path despite the non-causality being correctly diagnosed, a validation methodology that permits test-set leakage through checkpoint selection, and no config-management layer separating hyperparameters and system assumptions from source code. Each of these points is addressed individually, with an accept/dispute/defer judgment and reasoning, in `docs/EXTERNAL_REVIEW.md`.

## 7. Closing Assessment

The project reached a working, honestly-measured, and honestly-documented real-time BCI pipeline: an offline benchmark consistent with published literature on the same dataset, a real-time system with a quantified and explained accuracy gap rather than an unexplained one, and a full-featured monitoring application with a defensible engineering rationale behind its design choices — recorded, going forward, in `docs/decisions.md`. It is not a production neurotechnology system, and the documentation produced alongside it — this narrative, the decision log, the flow document, and the external review response — is intended to make that distinction explicit rather than implied, consistent with the engineering standard the project has tried to hold itself to throughout: report what was measured, explain why, and state plainly what remains unresolved.
